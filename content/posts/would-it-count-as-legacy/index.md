Most of what we do at Biz Hub is modernising legacy systems, so anything that mentions a government-wide look at legacy systems piques my interest. I [wrote about the Medicare agent](https://peti.to/posts/medicare-agent-doing-its-job/) earlier this month, and that incident is what prompted Home Affairs to order a [legacy stocktake](https://www.itnews.com.au/news/home-affairs-orders-gov-wide-legacy-system-stocktake-within-six-months-629313) across Commonwealth agencies, but I hadn't read the direction itself at the time. When I did (or rather got AI to read it for me), the definition of "legacy" turned out to be narrower than I'd assumed.

[The direction](https://www.protectivesecurity.gov.au/system/files/2026-09/pspf-direction-002-2026_0.pdf) counts a system as legacy when it meets "one or more criteria in both Category A and Category B". Category A is about the vendor: the product is end-of-life, or out of support, including any extended support from whoever made it. Category B is about the agency: the system is impractical to update or support, no longer cost-effective, above its acceptable level of risk, of diminishing use to the business, or getting in the way of its IT strategy. For a stocktake prompted by an agent wandering into a decades-old portal, starting with what the vendor will no longer patch seems pretty reasonable to me, and the Medicare portal would probably have made the list.

The trouble is that most of the systems that have caused me real grief over the years wouldn't have. The one I think of first is an integration we inherited during the Basix Housing Manager rewrite, which synced financial data between a property development platform and our database. It was supposed to be under support, but every developer who'd written it or knew how it worked had left, so working out what it did fell to us (mostly me). At a glance, it seemed to be working, but on closer inspection, it was dropping records and skipping some transaction types, and support staff usually found out weeks later when the numbers no longer matched. There was no diagram of how the pieces fit together and the behaviour lived in the code (I [wrote about it](https://peti.to/posts/apis-dont-fail-loudly-enough/) back in April, and I had no idea what Cosmos DB was when I started). It had a support arrangement, so if I've understood the Home Affairs direction right, it wouldn't have met Category A, even though the people supporting it couldn't explain what it did and were more or less hoping the problem would go away. I think an honest assessment would have put it well above any acceptable level of risk under Category B, but a system has to meet both, so it would never have got that far.

To be fair, RevenueSA's old tax system would have made it. It was an internal Java web application, still supported by the BAU team, but hard to maintain, and nobody wanted to invest any money in it, so it sat there in use and more or less stagnant. It ran on Java 5 (I think), which was well out of support by then, so it ticked Category A as well as Category B: impractical to update and of diminishing use. When the Job Accelerator Grant needed a public application portal in a hurry, it couldn't keep up, which is a big part of why we [rebuilt it](https://peti.to/posts/build-vs-buy-bill-moved/).

The Basix integration reminded me of [a piece I wrote for Biz Hub](https://www.bizhub.com.au/the-hidden-costs-of-shadow-it-why-quick-fixes-create-bigger-problems/) last year about shadow IT. The example I used was Jane (not her real name, but I've been on the receiving end of this at least once), who'd built an Access database years earlier that the business relied on every day, and who was six months from retirement and couldn't keep the grin off her face. Access was supported and so, for the time being, was the database, because Jane supported it, even though it lived on a laptop that could have died any day and nobody knew how to virtualise it. By the direction's definition, it wouldn't have been legacy until the day after her farewell morning tea.

I'm not the only one who's noticed this. Solution Minds, an advisory firm, [went back through 22 of its own engagements](https://www.sm-c.com.au/beyond-the-legacy-stocktake/) and found ownership was unclear in about three-quarters of them. Staff relied on manual work, spreadsheets, or workarounds in nearly all of them, and the system's age was documented in fewer than half. That's a consultancy that sells help with exactly this, counting its own clients, and it says itself that the sample leans towards problems and major change, so I'd take the numbers with a grain of salt. But it matches what I've seen: age is the easiest thing about a system to write down, and in my experience it's rarely the thing that ends up hurting.

If I were filling in one of these stocktakes, I'd add two columns. The first is who could explain what the system does, and whether they still work here. The second is what depends on it, since most of the damage the Basix integration did came from other people's numbers depending on it, with nothing raising an alert when it dropped records. The direction doesn't ask for either, but it does define a "system" to include "the governance framework in which it operates", and it leaves each agency to decide how deep its assessment goes, so there's nothing stopping anyone (not that I expect many will). Neither column changes whether a system meets the definition, but together they'd show which of the supported, patched rows are carrying the most risk, and those are the rows a stocktake built on this definition is most likely to leave off.

The direction only covers non-corporate Commonwealth entities. Those running Systems of Government Significance have to report on those systems by 31 December, and everyone else has until 31 March. If you're not one of them, the two columns are still a cheap exercise for any IT team, and if the answer to the first one is a single name, it's worth getting some of it written down while Jane is still around.
